\section{From one run to repeated evidence}

\begin{frame}{One run gives one possible morning}
  \small
  Last week, one parametric morning gave:
  \[
    5.78\text{ min with 16 agents}
    \quad\longrightarrow\quad
    4.15\text{ min with 17 agents},
  \]
  a reduction of 1.63 minutes in mean waiting time.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  The same model with new random outcomes gives:
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}crrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Repetition & 16 agents & 17 agents & Reduction\\
    \midrule
    1 & 2.23 & 1.44 & 0.79\\

    \bottomrule

  \raggedright
  \vspace{0.6em}

\begin{frame}{Define the quantity before estimating it}
  \small
  For simulated morning $r$ and staffing level $c$, let
  \[
    Y_c^{(r)}=\text{mean wait among callers arriving from 09:00 to 11:00}.
  \]
  Compare the two staffing levels within that morning:
  \[
    D_r=Y_{16}^{(r)}-Y_{17}^{(r)}.
  \]
  A positive $D_r$ is a reduction in waiting from adding the seventeenth agent.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Our target is
  \[
    \delta=\mathbb E[D_r],
  \]
  the expected reduction across mornings generated by the stated model.

  After $R$ independent simulated mornings, we estimate it with
  \[
    \bar D=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}D_r.
  \]
\end{frame}

\section{Estimate Monte Carlo error}



\begin{frame}{What 200 repetitions produced}
  \footnotesize
  Simulate $200$ independent mornings; paired 16/17 comparison:
  \vspace{0.5em}

  \centering
  \setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.45cm}rrr@{}}
    \toprule
      & 16 agents & 17 agents & Reduction $D_r$\\
    \midrule
    Mean wait (min) & 4.35 & 2.75 & 1.60\\
    Standard deviation (min) & 4.79 & 3.52 & 1.38\\
    10th percentile (min) & & & 0.12\\
    90th percentile (min) & & & 3.80\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \raggedright
  \vspace{0.8em}
  The 1.38-minute standard deviation and the 0.12--3.80 percentile range
  describe how $D_r$ varies between mornings. They do not measure uncertainty
  in the estimated mean $\bar D$.

  \scriptsize Seeds: arrival stream \texttt{20260301}; service stream \texttt{20260302}. Each morning starts empty at 09:00, as in Week 2.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Standard deviation and standard error answer different questions}
  \small
  Let $s_D$ be the sample standard deviation of $D_1,\ldots,D_R$.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{Standard deviation $s_D$}
      \[
        s_D=1.38\text{ min}
      \]
      Describes how the staffing benefit varies from one simulated morning to another.

      More repetitions estimate this spread better; they do not make mornings less variable.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{Standard error of the mean}
      \[
        \mathrm{SE}(\bar D)=\frac{s_D}{\sqrt R}
          =0.098\text{ min}.
      \]
      Describes the Monte Carlo precision of the estimated expected benefit $\bar D$.

      It decreases as the number of independent mornings grows.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem}
  \small
  The target and its estimate after $R$ independent mornings are
  \[
    \delta=\mathbb E[D_r],
    \qquad
    \bar D_R=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}D_r.
  \]

  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{Law of large numbers}
      \[
        \bar D_R \longrightarrow \delta
        \qquad\text{as }R\to\infty.
      \]
      It explains why the estimated mean settles toward the expected reduction.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{Central limit theorem}
      \[
        \frac{\bar D_R-\delta}{s_D/\sqrt R}
        \ \approx\ \mathcal N(0,1).
      \]
      It gives the approximate 95\% interval
      \[
        \bar D_R\pm1.96\frac{s_D}{\sqrt R}.
      \]
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  With $R=200$, the interval is $[1.41,1.80]$ minutes. It lies above zero.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Plan repetitions from a pilot run}
  \small
  If a pilot gives standard deviation $s_D$, target a confidence-interval half-width $h$ with
  \[
    R \approx \left(\frac{1.96s_D}{h}\right)^2.
  \]
  The first 30 repetitions gave $s_D=1.33$ minutes:

  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}cc@{}}
    \toprule
    Target half-width $h$ & Planned repetitions\\
    \midrule
    0.50 min & 28\\
    0.25 min & 110\\
    0.20 min & 171\\
    0.10 min & 683\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \raggedright
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Halving the target error requires roughly four times as many repetitions. Recheck the achieved half-width after the planned run because the pilot standard deviation is itself estimated.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pairing removes irrelevant noise}
  \small
  In the 200-repetition parametric experiment:
  \[
    \operatorname{corr}\!\left(Y_{16}^{(r)},Y_{17}^{(r)}\right)\approx0.99.
  \]
  Busy mornings are difficult under both staffing levels. Pairing uses that positive correlation.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
    \toprule
    Analysis of the staffing difference & Standard error & 95\% half-width\\
    \midrule
    Paired, using $D_r$ & 0.098 min & 0.192 min\\
    If treated as independent samples & 0.420 min & 0.824 min\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \raggedright
  \vspace{0.7em}
  The simulation effort is unchanged. The experimental design makes the comparison much more precise.
\end{frame}

\section{Check how far the conclusion travels}

\begin{frame}{Repeat the analysis with the empirical generator}
  \footnotesize
  Each row uses 200 independent paired repetitions.

  \centering

Check whether repetitions use fresh random draws, whether both staffing levels receive identical arrivals and services, whether the replication unit is the morning, and whether the reported standard error uses the paired differences. For each concern, propose a minimal reproducible check and its expected outcome.}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \textbf{Your work:}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item trace each claim to a specific line or stored output;
    \item calculate the expected result of every proposed check independently;
    \item run useful checks and retain them;
    \item record any unsupported claim or correction.

  A confident audit is not evidence until its checks have known, independently verified outcomes.

\section{Hands-on work and close}

\begin{frame}{Notebook route}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Turn the Week 2 experiment into a function that returns one morning-level result.
    \item Generate independent mornings and pair the 16- and 17-agent simulations.
    \item Inspect the distribution of the paired differences $D_r$.
    \item Separate standard deviation across mornings from standard error of the mean.
    \item Build and interpret an approximate 95\% confidence interval.
    \item Use a pilot run to plan the number of repetitions.
    \item Repeat the analysis with the whole-morning bootstrap generator.
    \item State the supported conclusion and the remaining model uncertainty.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What we take forward}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item One simulation run is one possible system history, not an estimate with known precision.
    \item Independent repetitions quantify operational variability and Monte Carlo error.
    \item Paired policy comparisons use the same random inputs and estimate staffing effects efficiently.
    \item Confidence intervals quantify finite-simulation error under a fixed model.
    \item More repetitions cannot repair unsupported input distributions or missing process mechanisms.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \textbf{Next week:} estimate and check the service-time distribution instead of assuming an exponential mean of six minutes.
\end{frame}
