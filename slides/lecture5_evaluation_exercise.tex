  In Lecture 4, we modelled how long an agent spends with a caller.
  The simulated queue served every request.

\section{Model patience}

\begin{frame}{What can we learn about patience?}
  Patience is how long a caller would wait before leaving the queue.

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.15cm,y=0.7cm,font=\small]
      \node[above] at (0,1.4) {Join at 0 s};
      \node[left] at (-1,0.95) {Caller 1};
      \node[left] at (-1,0) {Caller 2};
      \draw[densely dotted,gray] (0,-0.18) -- (0,1.35);
      \draw[very thick,AMU_dk2] (0,0.95) -- (40,0.95);
      \fill[AMU_dk2] (40,0.95) circle (2pt) node[right,text=black] {Leaves at 40 s};
      \draw[very thick,AMU_dk2] (0,0) -- (25,0);
      \fill[AMU_dk2] (25,0) circle (2pt) node[below,text=black] {Served at 25 s};
      \draw[thick,densely dotted,gray,->] (25.5,0) -- (40,0)
        node[right,text=black] {Patience unknown};
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  Caller 1's patience was 40 seconds. Caller 2 was willing to wait at
  least 25 seconds. We call that observation \textbf{right-censored}.

  Both calls contribute waiting time. Only caller 1 contributes an abandonment.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Check the abandonment rule by hand}
  \small
  One agent is free at time zero. Times are in seconds. A caller leaves
  if the earliest possible service start would come after their patience
  expires. At a tie, serve the caller.

  \vspace{0.65em}
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Caller & Arrives & Service takes & Patience\\
    \midrule
    1 &  0 & 80 & 10\\
    2 & 10 & 500 & 20\\
    3 & 80 &  10 &  5\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \vspace{0.65em}
  \raggedright
  Caller 1 starts immediately. Caller 2 would have to wait 70 seconds.
  When does caller 2 leave? When can caller 3 start?
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fit a constant rate of leaving}
  Assume exponential patience. A caller still waiting has a constant
  abandonment rate $\theta$ per second. Treat their patience and the
  time at which an agent becomes available as independent.

  \[
    \widehat{\theta} =
      \frac{\text{abandonments}}{\text{total seconds spent waiting}}
    = \frac{565}{217{,}565}.
  \]

  The fitted mean patience is $1/\widehat{\theta}\approx385$ seconds.
  The denominator includes waiting time from callers who were served.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \textbf{Discuss.} Callers who abandoned waited 66 seconds on average.
  Why would using only their waits give a different answer?
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How does the abandonment rate change with waiting time?}
  \small
  Split waits into successive minutes. A caller who leaves after 80 seconds
  contributes 60 waiting seconds to minute one, 20 to minute two, and one
  abandonment in minute two.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Time since joining & Abandoned & Seconds waiting & Rate per minute\\
    \midrule
    Minute 1 & 347 & 102,073 & 0.204\\
    Minute 2 & 132 & 53,362 & 0.148\\
    Minute 3 &  41 & 28,576 & 0.086\\
    Minute 4 &  21 & 15,356 & 0.082\\
    From 4 minutes & 24 & 18,198 & 0.079\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \vspace{0.65em}
  \raggedright
  The rate drops through minute three, then stays close to
  0.08 per minute of time spent waiting.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Choose three patience rates}
  \small
  Fit the candidates on 1--14 November. Score them on the 3,581
  requests with known outcomes on 15--30 November.
  The log likelihood uses each wait and how it ended. The table shows
  the gain over one rate.

  \vspace{0.55em}
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
    \toprule
    Model and change points & Log-likelihood gain\\
    \midrule

\end{frame}

\section{Simulate}

\begin{frame}{Arrival model A: the Week 3 and 4 generator}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Set an average arrival rate for the 09:00--11:00 window.
    \item Multiply it by a random factor drawn once per morning.
      This lets some mornings be busier than others.
    \item Draw exponential gaps at that morning's rate until 11:00.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Earlier fits counted entries to the phone menu. Here we fit the
  same generator to the number of agent requests on each fit morning.
  The average rate is 2.60 requests per minute.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Hold arrivals and service fixed}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Generate a morning of arrivals with model A.
    \item Draw each caller's service duration from the lognormal model
      from Lecture 4, fitted to the original durations.
    \item Draw how long each caller is willing to wait.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Start at 09:00 with idle agents and a single queue in arrival order.
  Share arrivals and service times across the patience models so we
  can compare the outcomes on the same mornings.
\end{frame}

\section{Use arrival timestamps}

\begin{frame}{Fit an exponential to the observed arrival gaps}
  Take the difference between successive request times on each morning.
  For example, three recorded times give two gaps.

  \[
    \text{09:00:12}\ \xrightarrow{\ 24\text{ s}\ }
    \text{09:00:36}\ \xrightarrow{\ 58\text{ s}\ }\ \text{09:01:34}
  \]

  Fit the exponential's mean to the average of the 3,105 complete gaps
  from the ten fit mornings.

  \[
    \text{Fitted mean gap} =
      \frac{71{,}445\text{ seconds}}{3{,}105\text{ gaps}}
      \approx 23.01\text{ seconds.}
  \]

  The fitted rate is 2.608 requests per minute. The earlier count fit
  gave 2.596.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What does the daily factor add?}
  \small
  Use the rate fitted to timestamps. First use it every morning.
  Then multiply it by a fresh morning factor, as in Week 3.
  Compare both with observations from 15--30 November.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \centering
  \setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Arrival model & Mean gap & Gap SD & Morning count SD\\
    \midrule
    Observed 15--30 Nov & 23.9 s & 24.7 s & 38.5\\
    Earlier counts + daily factor & 23.3 s & 23.6 s & 41.5\\
    Fitted gaps, fixed rate & 23.0 s & 23.0 s & 17.5\\
    Fitted gaps + daily factor & 23.2 s & 23.5 s & 41.7\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \vspace{0.55em}
  \raggedright
  The gap statistics are similar across the fits. The daily factor
  brings the variation in total requests per morning closer to the data.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Arrival model B: resample the same morning two ways}
  Pick one of the ten fit mornings at random. Use it for both versions
  below. Each repetition draws again from all ten mornings.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{15-minute counts.} Keep the count in each interval.
      Place that many arrivals uniformly inside it.
    \item \textbf{Timestamps.} Replay the recorded times in order.
      Keep both requests when they arrive in the same second.
  \end{itemize}

  Both versions keep the same quarter-hour totals. The gaps between
  requests can differ.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Does the arrival model change the staffing result?}
  \small
  The two resampling versions share the same 600 sampled mornings,
  service times, and patience draws. Timing within the quarter-hours changes.

  \vspace{0.65em}
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    \multicolumn{4}{@{}l@{}}{Share served within 60 seconds}\\
    Arrival input & 9 agents & 10 agents & Gain\\
    \midrule
    A: Counts + daily factor & 70.6\% & 81.4\% & 10.8 pp\\
    A: Fitted gaps, fixed rate & 70.5\% & 82.1\% & 11.6 pp\\
    A: Fitted gaps + daily factor & 70.2\% & 81.1\% & 10.9 pp\\
    B: 15-minute counts & 65.8\% & 77.7\% & 11.9 pp\\
    B: Timestamps & 65.6\% & 77.8\% & 12.2 pp\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \vspace{0.65em}
  \raggedright
  Within Model B, the gain with timestamps is 0.27 percentage points
  higher. The paired 95\% Monte Carlo interval for the difference is
  $[-0.16,\;0.69]$ points.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Ten agents meet the illustrative 75\% target across all versions.
\end{frame}
